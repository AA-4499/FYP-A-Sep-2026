2. SPRINT PROGRESS 
2.1 Product overview and dataset preparation  
Smart Diabetes Digital Twin provides role -based workspaces for patients, doctors and administrators. The 
prototype includes patient information, longitudinal glucose monitoring, glucose forecasting, knowledge -
graph exploration and digital -twin interface components. A shared patient identifier links summary 
information with longitudinal measurements, supporting consistent patient selection across the application.  
Dataset preparation covered 109 source workbooks containing 112,379 rows. Of these, 112,287 rows 
contained parseable continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) values, 2,999 contained parseable dietary 
entries and 2,366 contained parseable insulin entries. These counts describe the source records; the number 
of workbooks does not necessarily represent the number of unique patients. Missing dietary or insulin 
entries were treated as unrecorded information rather than evidence of fasting or no medication.  
KNIME workflows were developed to prepare the patient_summary and patient_detail datasets. For 
patient_summary, categorical fields were separated, grouped and recombined to consolidate repeated 
information. The output columns were then organised and renamed for subsequent database integration.  
For patient_detail, records from individual patient workbooks were combined into a common structure. 
Patient numbers were added, inconsistent column names were standardised and numeric conversion was 
applied where required. The resulting dataset retained timestamps, glucose measurements, dietary entries 
and medication fields, providing the foundation for patient -specific monitoring and forecasting.  
Figures 40-42 show the preprocessing workflows and a sample output. The output distinguishes recorded 
measurements from missing entries and preserves separate fields for different glucose measurements and 
medication types.

2.2 Architecture and Access Foundation 
Smart Diabetes Digital Twin uses a React and TypeScript frontend built with Vite. The frontend provides 
role-based workspaces and interfaces for patient information, glucose monitoring, forecasting and 
knowledge-graph exploration. Supabase supports authentication, account profiles and access to patient 
summary and longitudinal records. A Flask backend provides API endpoints for application services and 
module integration.  
The authentication workflow links user accounts to their profiles and, where applicable, patient numbers. 
Platform-assigned roles determine the appropriate workspace. The Doctor knowledge -graph API also 
checks patient assignments before returning patient -specific graph information. Access control therefore 
includes both role handling and checks on the patient records requested.  
The knowledge -graph backend retrieves medical concepts and relationships from Neo4j Aura. Database 
credentials are managed on the server. The graph integration combines this medical knowledge with 
authorised patient information to support the Doctor Knowledge Graph workspace.  
Glucose model training is performed offline, producing model checkpoints, evaluation results and exported 
inference assets. The Prediction Lab supports browser -based inference and saved forecast previews, with 
an additional backend forecasting route available through configuration. Forecast -source labels distinguish 
model-generated results from saved previews. The backend forecasting route remains subject to 
deployment verification .

2.3 AI Training and Initial Forecasting Results 
A PyTorch Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) model was developed to predict the next 15 -minute glucose 
reading using the preceding six hours of data. Each input sequence contains 24 time steps and nine features 
representing glucose, meal and insulin context, and cyclic time -of-day information. The model predicts the 
change from the latest glucose measurement, which is then added to that measurement to obtain the 
forecast. 
The model used a hidden size of 64, a batch size of 128 and a learning rate of 0.001, with the random seed 
set to 2026. Training was configured for a maximum of 30 epochs and completed after 29 epochs.  
Data were divided chronologically within each source record. The first 80% formed the training period, 
with its final portion reserved for validation, while the remaining 20% formed the test period. This 
approach evaluated predictions on later observations  from the same records. Performance on entirely 
unseen patients remains to be evaluated.

During development, a timestamp -alignment issue was identified in recursive forecasting. A newly

subsequent conditional -model experiment. Regression tests were added for the identified behaviours.  
The initial GRU checkpoint was retained separately from the experimental conditional model, allowing 
further model development without replacing the existing forecasting model.

2.4.3 Scenario Sensitivity and Current Limitations  
Scenario tests examined how forecasts changed when dietary and insulin inputs were varied. A 60 g food -
mass input produced a negative signed peak response in approximately 80% of the evaluated cases, while a 
2 IU insulin bolus produced positive peak responses in all evaluated cases. These responses raised 
concerns about the model’s suitability for physiological scenario simulation.  
The tests demonstrate sensitivity to input changes but do not establish causal treatment effects. Changes 
across the full forecast curve must also be considered, as a small difference at the final time point can 
conceal larger intermediate changes.  
The conditional GRU therefore remains experimental. Further investigation of event encoding, data quality 
and response behaviour is required before using it for meal or insulin intervention simulation. Its 
checkpoint remains separate from the initial forecasting model.

3.3 Critical Review of the Forecasting Increment 
3.3.1 Forecasting Performance and Interpretation  
The initial GRU achieved a next -step MAE of 3.81 mg/dL, compared with 5.93 mg/dL for the persistence 
baseline. This demonstrates improved 15 -minute forecasting on the evaluated chronological test windows. 
However, this result does not represent the accuracy of a recursively generated 6 -hour or 12 -hour forecast, 
where errors can accumulate across prediction steps.  
The conditional GRU addressed longer -horizon forecasting by directly predicting a glucose trajectory. On 
the same 108 forecast starting points, its 12 -hour trajectory MAE was 24.77 mg/dL, compared with 29.70 
mg/dL for the initial GRU with corrected recursion and 34.64 mg/dL for persistence. The lower error 
supports further development, although statistical significance, performance on unseen patients and clinical 
usefulness have not been established.  
The separate patient -summary experiment showed modest improvements when baseline information was 
included. Its 1,422 overlapping trajectories came from known records and therefore do not represent 
independent patients. The expanded configuration also included undated laboratory measurements whose 
availability at the forecast time could not be confirmed. Consequently, the seven -field baseline 
configuration provides a more defensible basis for continued development, while the expanded 
configuration remains re trospective.  
3.3.2 Limitations of Scenario Simulation  
The sensitivity experiments identified an important limitation: dietary inputs frequently produced 
downward forecast changes, while insulin bolus inputs produced upward changes. These responses show 
that the model uses the event inputs, but they do not support interpreting its outputs as reliable 
physiological intervention effects.  
Possible explanations include incomplete event recording, differences between insulin administration types 
and treatment decisions made in response to existing glucose levels. These factors require further 
investigation before the model can support meaningful meal or insulin scenarios.  
The conditional model therefore remains an experimental forecasting component. Its current results do not 
support insulin -dose selection or individual treatment recommendations.

5. LESSONS LEARNED AND FUTURE PLAN  
5.1 Reflection on Sprint 1 
Sprint 1 demonstrated the importance of defining completion clearly. Dividing work into broad modules 
helped allocate responsibilities, but did not provide enough detail for tracking progress. Backlog items 
need specific outputs, owners, estimates and acceptance criteria so that implementation and outstanding 
work can be assessed consistently.  
The client/supervisor’s feedback also showed that interface appearance and functional completeness must 
be developed together. Revising individual screens improved presentation, but a usable application also 
requires connected navigation, consistent patient selection and complete user journeys. Earlier 
walkthroughs of the shared application would help identify these issues before formal reviews.  
The forecasting work highlighted the need to clarify the modelling objective before training. Prediction 
targets, chronological sequence preparation and evaluation methods should be agreed early. Improved 
forecasting accuracy also does not establish reliable treatment effects, as demonstrated by the conditional 
model’s unexpected dietary and insulin responses.  
Knowledge-graph development showed that constructing relationships is only part of the work. Source 
verification, relationship review and reproducible database population are necessary before those 
relationships can support analysis.  
Finally, progress records should be maintained throughout development. Regular Jira updates, recorded 
test results and brief notes from verbal feedback would reduce the effort needed to reconstruct progress 
when preparing the report.
